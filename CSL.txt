\section{Abstract}

Due to the law of increasing marginal costs, the marginal cost of life should be lower in low-life-expectancy countries than in high-life-expectancy countries, all else being equal.
To assess this, I estimated the marginal cost of life curve.
I assume that a government would choose both life expectancy and the price of life in its country corresponding to the intersection of a marginal cost of life curve and a marginal benefit of life curve.
To estimate the marginal cost of life curve, I used two-stage least squares.
I downloaded data from three sources: the Tufts Medical Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA) Registry, the World Bank, and the US Federal Reserve.
I used Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita as an instrument because it should correlate with shifts in the marginal benefit of life curve but not the marginal cost of life curve.
Also, I also controlled for several variables that could correlate with shifts in the marginal cost of life curve.
The marginal cost of life is strongly increasing.
With 95\% confidence, when life expectancy increases by one year, all else being equal, the marginal cost of life increases by between
9.51\% and 
37.1\%.
The marginal cost of life is far lower in some countries than others.
For example, I predict that, in 
2015,
while the marginal cost of life was
66,400 
international dollars per QALY in
Japan,
it was
64.3
international dollars per QALY in
Lesotho.

\section{Introduction}

It is hard to spend time on the internet and not see an ad along the following lines:
``for just [a small amount of money], you can save a life in [a developing country]''.
Why is it so cost-effective to save lives in developing countries?
The answer, I believe, is due to intersecting marginal-cost-of-life and marginal-benefit-of-life curves.
Individuals and governments, implicitly or explicitly, use these curves to make decisions about their health.
As Schelling suggested, ``we nearly all want our lives extended and are probably willing to pay for it'' \autocite{Schelling_1968}.
These curves determine not only individual differences in health outcomes, but global differences in health outcomes.

Here, I estimate the marginal-cost-of-life (MCL) curve, which is the relationship between life expectancy and the cost to extend life expectancy.
Here are four possible uses for my results.
First, a government could use the MCL curve to estimate how much it would cost to achieve a certain increase or decrease in life expectancy.
Second, a government could use the MCL curve to inform its cost-effectiveness threshold.
A cost-effectiveness threshold is the cut-off that a government uses to decide which treatments to pay for.
In fact, some researchers are already using price of life data to inform cost-effectiveness thresholds \autocite{Claxton_2015}.
Third, donors could use the MCL curve to inform decisions about foreign aid.
Individuals/governments could use the MCL curve to predict how much it would cost to extend life expectancy of other individuals/citizens of other countries, respectively.
Finally, international figures for the price of life reveal both self-regarding and other-regarding values.
Individuals make a trade-off between spending money to extend their own life, spending money to extend the lives of others, and spending money in other ways.
Likewise, governments make a trade-off between spending money to extend the lives of their own citizens, spending money to extend the lives of citizens of other countries, and spending money in other ways.
Thus, through revealed preferences, international figures for the price of life reveal both self-regarding and other-regarding values.

This last point about revealed preferences raises the question: to what extent do rich individuals/governments have a duty to pay for treatments for other individuals/citizens of other countries, respectively?
This is an ethical question that is outside the scope of this paper.
On one hand, it is upsetting (to me) that rich individuals/governments spend so much money on cost-ineffective treatments for themselves/their own citizens, when they could spend the same money on much more cost-effective treatments for others individuals/citizens of other countries, respectively.
Indeed, effective altruists argue that one should do the good that one can \autocite{MacAskill_2015}.
On the other hand, given the economic inequality in our world, it is not surprising that there is inequality in the quantity of life, which is a good just like any other.
So why is inequality in the quantity of life so much more upsetting (to me) than inequality in the distribution of any other good?
Perhaps this is because one's quantity of life is perhaps the most important good, in that living is a requirement for the enjoyment of any other good.
In other words, one's quantity of life is the most fundamental capability, to use the terminology of Amartya Sen \autocite{Sen_2008}.

My results contribute to the literature because, to my knowledge, no researcher has estimated the MCL curve.
Instead, researchers have focused on either predicting the price of life, or estimating the relationship between life expectancy and individual categories of treatments.
Several authors have estimated the price of life in individual countries \autocites{Claxton_2015,Edney_2022}, and some have compared the price of life across different countries \autocites{Ochalek_2018,Woods_2016}.
However, these authors do not directly estimate the marginal-cost relationship between the price of life and life expectancy.
Other authors have estimated the relationship between life expectancy and individual categories of treatments, e.g. healthcare spending, water quality/sanitation, nutrition, etc. \autocite{Alsan_2019,Fogel_2004,Gallet_2017}.
In contrast, my results are more general, because I use cost-effectiveness ratios for all categories of treatments.

Because this model is a helpful framing device, I will focus on explaining the model before I review the literature in depth.
This is largely the same model used by Johannesson \autocite{Johannesson_1995}.

Here, I estimate the marginal cost of life curve, which, roughly, is the relationship between life expectancy and the cost to extend life expectancy.
To be more specific, I define terms formally below. 

\textbf{Quality-adjusted life year (QALY)}: a health improvement that one values as much as an extra year of life at full health.
This measure is necessarily subjective.

\textbf{Health-adjusted life expectancy}: traditional life expectancy is how many years which one predicts a baby born in a country will live for, on average.
In this paper, I use health-adjusted life expectancy, which is life expectancy measured in QALYs rather than years.

\textbf{Treatment}: any good a citizen or government could purchase to increase a citizen's life expectancy.
This is a broad definition that includes not only healthcare treatments, but also nutrition, water quality and sanitation, and much more. 

\textbf{Cost-effectiveness ratio} or the \textbf{Price of life}: how much a treatment costs in units of currency per QALY.
For example, buying rehydration fluid for a small child with life-threatening diarrhea would have a low cost-effectiveness ratio: rehydration fluid has a low cost, and the child could live for many more years.
On the other hand, paying for radiation therapy for an elderly person with cancer would have a high cost-effectiveness ratio: radiation therapy has a high cost, and even if the treatment were successful, the elderly person would probably not live much longer.
I describe a cost-effectiveness ratio as a ``price of life'' because a price is the cost per unit of a good, and for a cost-effectiveness ratio, that good is a QALY.
Note that, perhaps counter-intuitively, a treatment with a comparatively high cost-effectiveness would have a comparatively low cost-effectiveness ratio.
This is one reason that, unlike in the literature, I use the term ``price of life'' instead of ``cost-effectiveness ratio''.

\textbf{Cost-effectiveness threshold} or the \textbf{Marginal benefit of life}: in a country, the cost effectiveness ratio of the treatment with the maximum cost-effectiveness ratio among the treatments that a typical citizen or the government is willing to pay for.
For example, the cost-effectiveness threshold of England and Wales' National Health Service is currently between £20,000 and £30,000 per QALY.
This is surprisingly close to the value I estimated for the UK; see \autoref{fig:intersection_example}.

A citizen or government can use a cost-effectiveness threshold to decide which treatments to buy to increase their life expectancy.
Although this seems callous, most public health agencies use such cost-effectiveness thresholds to make healthcare decisions.

I call a cost-effectiveness threshold the ``marginal benefit of life'' because it is the value a citizen or government assigns to a QALY.
For example, if a citizen or government is willing to pay for treatments that cost no more than \$25,000 per QALY, the citizen or government must value a QALY at \$25,000.

\textbf{Marginal cost of life}: For a country, the marginal cost of life is the cost-effectiveness ratio of the treatment any citizen needs with the minimum cost-effectiveness ratio.

Below, I describe a model of how a typical citizen or government makes decisions about life expectancy.

To explain the model, I imagine an impartial government purchasing treatments on behalf of their citizens.
I discuss how to extend this model to individual citizens below.
Imagine an impartial government paying for treatments for their citizens.
By impartial, I mean that the government would prioritize the most cost-effective treatments without regard for which citizen would receive them.
For the model, I use the following concepts:

\textbf{Marginal cost of life curve}: the relationship between life expectancy and the marginal cost of life.

By the law of increasing marginal costs, the marginal cost of life curve should be increasing.
This is for the following reason.
Because they are impartial, the government would prioritize the most cost-effective treatments first.
To increase life expectancy, the government could raise their cost-effectiveness threshold.
Because the government would pay for more treatments, mortality rates would decrease, so life expectancy would increase.
Also, because the government would pay for all possible treatments with a cost-effectiveness ratio below the new threshold, the marginal cost of further treatments would increase to the new threshold.
Thus, there would be a positive relationship between life expectancy (the ``quantity'' of life) and the marginal cost of life (the ``price'' of life).
As a metaphor, consider picking fruit from a tree.
It is not very costly to pick low-hanging fruit, but once the low-hanging fruit is picked, it becomes more costly to pick fruit higher up in the tree.
This aligns with the law of increasing marginal costs.

Also, note that treatments mediate the marginal-cost relationship between life expectancy and the price of life.
That is, a shift along the marginal cost curve, as I discuss above, is caused by the increased provision of certain treatments.
Treatments fall into categories such as healthcare spending, nutrition, and water quality/sanitation.
So, if one held fixed the level of treatments, one would not observe any relationship between life expectancy and the price of life, at least on the marginal-cost side.

\textbf{Marginal benefit of life curve}: the relationship between life expectancy and the marginal benefit of life.
By the law of decreasing marginal benefits, the marginal benefit of life curve is probably slightly decreasing.
This is for the following reason.
Consider how a government would change their cost-effectiveness threshold if life expectancy exogenously increased.
Because this increase is exogenous, other factors, such as the government's ability to pay for treatments, would stay the same.
Although most citizens would desire additional life as much as before, some elderly people, who would have lived more complete lives, might desire additional life less.
In response, the government might slightly decrease its cost-effective threshold, which represents the marginal benefit of life. 
Thus, there might be a slightly negative relationship between life expectancy (the ``quantity'' of life) and the marginal benefit of life (the ``price'' of life).
This aligns with the law of decreasing marginal benefits.

The government's ability to pay for life would determine the government's marginal benefit of life curve.
Two effects can cause a marginal benefits curve to shift: the ``income effect'' and the ``substitution effect'', but in this case, only the income effect is relevant.
Generally, the ``income effect'' means that, if someone's income increased, the person would have a higher ability to pay, and thus, willingness to pay, for a good.
Due to the income effect, if per-capita income in the country increased, but other factors, such as life expectancy, remained the same, the government would have a higher ability to pay, and thus, willingness to pay, for treatments.
So, if per capita income in a country increased, the marginal benefit of life curve would shift upwards.
The ``substitution effect'' means that, if the price of a substitute good decreased, someone might choose to purchase a substitute good instead, so their willingness to pay for the original good would decrease.
In this case, substitute goods would include goods related to the enjoyment, but not duration, of life.

Thus, there should be a strong relationship between cost-effectiveness thresholds and GDP per capita.
In fact, the WHO recommends setting cost-effectiveness thresholds to some multiple of per-capita income \autocite{Hutubessy_2003}.
Moreover, the cost-effectiveness thresholds of most countries aligns with this advice \autocite{Iino_2022}.

Note that, in using the term marginal benefit of life, I am making a descriptive, not a prescriptive, claim.
I am only describing how individuals value their own life, and how impartial governments value the lives of their own citizens.
In fact, I do not believe that the social value of life is higher for someone who has a higher ability to pay for life.

A rational government would choose a level of funding corresponding to where the marginal benefit and marginal cost curves intersect.
This is for the following reason.
If the government's marginal benefit of life were greater than the marginal cost of life, the government would pay for more treatments.
If the government's marginal benefit of life were less than the marginal cost of life, the government would pay for fewer treatments.
At equilibrium, the government would then choose a level of funding corresponding to where the marginal benefit and marginal cost curves intersect.
The position of this intersection would correspond to a certain life expectancy and a certain price of life.
I plotted an example for the UK in \autoref{fig:intersection_example}.

To extend this model to citizens, I assume that individual variation within a country and year will average out.
Not only impartial governments can pay for treatments.
Citizens pay for some of their own treatments, and citizens vary in their ability to pay for treatments.
Therefore, within a country and year, some citizens should have a comparatively high life expectancy and price of life, while other citizens should have a comparatively low life expectancy and price of life.
However, a typical citizen will have roughly average income, and thus, roughly average life expectancy and a roughly average price of life.
Thus, by considering averages within a country-year, I can ignore individual-level variation.

\section{Literature}

I will organize the literature review around three topics.

First, I will discuss broad literature on the various determinants of life expectancy.
Second, I will discuss the normative question of how governments should value their citizen's lives.
Third, I will discuss methods of choosing cost-effectiveness thresholds.

On the supply side, many categories of treatments increase life expectancy:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Healthcare spending \autocite{Gallet_2017}
\item
  Nutrition \autocite{Fogel_2004}
\item  
  Water sanitation/quality \autocite{Alsan_2019}
\item
  Reductions in air pollution \autocite{Caiazzo_2013}
\end{itemize}

On the demand side, the Preston curve is the relationship between GDP per capita and life expectancy \autocite{Preston_1975}.
Preston found that the relationship was non-linear, with a lower slope for high-income countries.
Also, Preston found that the curve has shifted over time, as life expectancy increases at all income levels.
The causality of this relationship is debated.
While the demand for health is higher at higher incomes, it is also possible that better health causes increases in GDP \autocite{Acemoglu_2007}.

Governments value their citizen's lives in at least three conflicting ways.
In addition to cost-effectiveness thresholds, which I discuss above, governments also use a value of a statistical life (VSL) and individual foregone earnings.
If a government used a cost-effectiveness threshold, it would value QALYs of all citizens' lives equally.
Cost-effectiveness thresholds are commonly used by health economists.
Compare this to a VSL, which is how much the government values the remainder of a citizen's life \autocite{Viscusi_2003}.
VSLs are commonly used by environmental economists.
If a government used a value of a statistical life, it would value the remainder of all citizens' lives equally.
So, unlike if it used a value of a statistical life, a government that used a cost-effectiveness threshold would prioritize saving the young, who, on average, have many more years to live, over saving the old, who do not.
Individual foregone earnings are commonly used in law \autocite{Feinberg_2005}.
So, unlike if it used a value of a statistical life, a government that used individual foregone earnings would prioritize saving those with high incomes over saving those with low incomes.

There are several proposed methods for governments to choose cost-effectiveness thresholds.
The first is to set cost-effectiveness thresholds to the average citizen's willingness-to-pay for a QALY \autocite{Iino_2022}.
The second is to set cost-effectiveness thresholds to some multiple of per-capita income, with the expectation of a positive relationship between per-capita income and the average willingness-to-pay for a QALY \autocite{Hutubessy_2003}.
The third is to set cost-effectiveness thresholds to balance the growth rates of life expectancy and health expenditure \autocite{Pichon_Riviere_2023}.
The fourth is to set cost-effectiveness thresholds to the equilibrium price of life \autocite{Claxton_2015}.

This last method, setting cost-effectiveness thresholds to the equilibrium price of life, is closely related to the results here. 
The method makes sense only if the government wishes to maintain, rather than increase or decrease, its current life expectancy.
It has been pioneered by Claxton, who estimated the price of life in the UK \autocite{Claxton_2015}.
Claxton's methodology has been replicated in few other countries \autocite{Edney_2022}.
More interestingly, Woods et al. \autocite{Woods_2016} and Ochalek et al. \autocite{Ochalek_2018} have extended the analysis internationally.
So, it is worth comparing their agenda to mine.
There are three key differences.
First, since I  take the equilibrium price of life directly from published cost-effectiveness ratios, I do not need to indirectly estimate it using health expenditure.
Second, I do not limit my analysis only to healthcare treatments, as many treatments fall outside the category of traditional healthcare.
Third, my goal is to estimate an empirical marginal cost of life curve, while the goal of Claxton et al. is to make a normative claim about how governments should set cost-effectiveness thresholds.

\section{Methods}

The marginal cost of life curve and the marginal benefit of life curve mutually determine two variables: life expectancy and the price of life.
Thus, these two variables are endogenous.
Therefore, I used two-stage least squares to estimate the marginal cost of life curve.
I used GDP per capita as an instrumental variable because it should correlate with shifts of the marginal benefit curve but not the marginal cost curve.
I discuss my reasoning for below.

I downloaded data from four sources: the World Health Organization, the Tufts Medical Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA) Registry, the World Bank, and the US Federal Reserve.

I downloaded data on health-adjusted life expectancy from the World Health Organization \autocite{WHO}.

To obtain price of life data, I downloaded data about the cost-effectiveness of healthcare treatments from the CEA Registry.
This registry is a ``comprehensive database of greater than 10,000 cost-utility analyses on a wide variety of diseases and treatments published from 1976 to the present'' \autocite{CEA_registry}.
As of when I downloaded the data, the most recent study was published in 2024.
For each study, Tufts Medical reports the following variables:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  the publication year
\item
  the country (or countries) where the study participants live
\item
  the currency used to calculate costs.
\item
  the year of currency used to calculate costs.
\item
  a price in local historical currency per QALY
\end{itemize}

I logged both GDP per capita and prices because all values are positive, and the distributions are right-skewed.

For each country and year (country-year), I used the average of log prices published in that country-year as a rough estimate of the log price of life.
Of course, no author who published such a price intended to estimate the price of life in a country and year.
In fact, each author was simply analyzing the cost-effectiveness of a treatment the author was interested in and could obtain data about.
Still, the average of the log of published prices in each country and year might be close to the log price of life, for three reasons.
First, unless they use retrospective data, a researcher can only study treatments that people in a country and year can still benefit from.
Second, researchers can most easily study treatments that are typical in a country and year.
Typical treatments should be cost-effective because citizens and governments ideally choose the most cost-effective treatments.
Third, if a researcher found a low price for an uncommon treatment, a publisher would be more likely to publish the researcher's results.
Such a low price would be close to the marginal price of life.

I only used some prices.
I excluded prices from studies of participants from more than one country.
I also excluded zero or negative prices because a price must be positive by my definition.

In \autoref{sec:prices_appendix}, I discuss further choices I made in processing prices, and test against alternatives for robustness.

To obtain the main input variable, life expectancy, the instrumental variable, GDP per capita, and control variables, I downloaded data from the World Bank \autocite{World_Bank}.

I report further details about these variables in \autoref{sec:WDI_variables}.

I used log GDP per capita as an instrumental variable because an exogenous change in GDP per capita would cause the marginal benefit of life curve, but not the marginal cost of life curve, to shift.
An exogenous increase in GDP per capita would cause the marginal benefit curve to shift upwards, because, as I discussed above, countries with a higher GDP per capita have a higher ability to pay, and thus, willingness to pay, for life.
All else being equal, an exogenous change in GDP per capita would not cause the marginal cost of life curve to shift, because a change in GDP per capita would not change the cost of any given treatment.
Here, I am referring to the real cost of treatments in terms of inputs (like hours of skilled labor and equipment), not the currency cost, which I address below.
The only possible exception I can imagine is that countries with higher GDP per capita might have a more educated populace, who might be able to cost-effectively manage some aspects of their health without professionals.
However, I considered controlling for levels of education, and many other variables possibly related to the marginal cost of life.
Moreover, any relationship that exists between GDP per capita and the marginal cost of life would be negligible compared to the very strong relationship between GDP per capita and the marginal benefit of life.

I controlled for several variables that could be correlated with shifts of the marginal cost of life curve.
I discuss this further in \autoref{sec:control_variables}.
I also included year fixed effects as a control variable.

To compare prices between different countries, I used PPPs for GDP, and to deflate prices, I used GDP deflators.
I downloaded deflator data from the US Federal Reserve \autocite{FRED}.
To check for robustness, I repeated my calculations using different sets of exchange rates and deflators, and report the results in \autoref{sec:price_adjustments_appendix}.

For the regression, I discarded rows where the value of any variable is missing.
In general, more data is missing for developing countries than for developed countries.
However, unless developed countries are different from developing countries in a way I did not control for, this should not bias my results.

To handle country-level variation, I had three options: fixed effects, random effects, or pooling.
For the main analysis, I pooled countries.
I clustered standard errors by country, such that standard errors should be robust to heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation within a country.
In \autoref{sec:specification_appendix}, I report the results from testing for robustness to alternate model specifications.

\section{Results}

I found that the marginal cost of life is strongly increasing.
With 95\% confidence, when life expectancy increases by one year, the marginal cost of life increases by between 
9.51\% and 
37.1\%.
Life expectancy is by far the most statistically significant predictor.
I report more detailed results in \autoref{sec:full_regression_results}.

In \autoref{tab:cost_of_life_table} and \autoref{fig:cost_of_life_map}, I show that the marginal cost of life is far lower in low-life-expectancy countries than in high-life-expectancy countries.
For example, I predict that, in 
2015,
while the marginal cost of life was
66,400 
international dollars per QALY in
Japan,
it was
64.3
international dollars per QALY in
Lesotho.

I also conducted analyses to check the validity of these results.
In \autoref{sec:stationarity_tests}, I report the results from testing for stationarity in the random effects model.
From these tests, I conclude that stationarity is not a problem in the random effects model.

From these tests, I conclude that there is no evidence that the fixed effects model and the random effects model are inconsistent.
These results apply equally to the random effects model and the pooled model, because the random effects model is simply a more efficient estimator of the pooled model.

\section{Discussion}

Due to the law of increasing marginal costs, the marginal cost of life should be lower in low-life-expectancy countries than in high-life-expectancy countries, all else being equal.
To assess this, I estimated the marginal cost of life curve.
I assume that a government would choose both life expectancy and the price of life in its country corresponding to the intersection of a marginal cost of life curve and a marginal benefit of life curve.
To estimate the marginal cost of life curve, I used two-stage least squares.
I downloaded data from three sources: the Tufts Medical Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA) Registry, the World Bank, and the US Federal Reserve.
I used Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita as an instrument because it should correlate with shifts in the marginal benefit of life curve but not the marginal cost of life curve.
Also, I also controlled for several variables that could correlate with shifts in the marginal cost of life curve.
The marginal cost of life is strongly increasing.
With 95\% confidence, when life expectancy increases by one year, all else being equal, the marginal cost of life increases by between
9.51\% and 
37.1\%.
The marginal cost of life is far lower in some countries than others.
For example, I predict that, in 
2015,
while the marginal cost of life was
66,400 
international dollars per QALY in
Japan,
it was
64.3
international dollars per QALY in
Lesotho.

There are several caveats to these results, all of which I discuss in more detail above or in the appendices.
I have summarized these caveats below, ordering them roughly from the most severe to least severe.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  There is very little variation within countries, preventing me from estimating the marginal cost curve with country fixed effects.
  This means that the results here could also be explained by a control variable which I did not account for.
  This control variable would need to vary between countries but not much over time, be correlated with modeled life expectancy, and causally affect the price of life.
  Still, I did my best to include a wide array of control variables, even conducting automated searches through the World Development Indicators for candidates.
\item 
  The prices adjusted by Tufts readers seem to contain less variation between countries and years than the published and peer reviewed figures.
  It would be good to better understand the cause of this discrepancy.
\item
  It is difficult to disentangle the effect of modeled life expectancy from the effect of some control variables with which it is highly collinear.
  Still, including these collinear controls does not affect the central estimate for the modeled life expectancy coefficient.
\item
  The exclusion restriction, that GDP per capita is uncorrelated with the marginal cost of life, is not unassailable.
  Still, any relationship that exists would be negligible compared to the very strong relationship between GDP per capita and the marginal benefit of life.
\item
  I could not precisely tailor the exchange rates I used to the equalize the real costs of any given treatment between countries.
  However, the results are still robust to sets of exchange rates for which there is sufficient data.
\item
  The average of published prices is only a rough estimate of the underlying price of life.
\end{itemize}

The central estimate using reader-adjusted prices is still practically high, and the confidence interval fully overlaps my main estimate.
Thus, I am not overly worried about this discrepancy.

\section{Specification robustness tests}
\label{sec:specification_appendix}

There were a number of decisions I had to make in order choose a model specification.

First, I had to decide how to account for country-level variation.
Ideally, I would use fixed effects by country.
However, the resulting confidence interval for the life expectancy coefficient was very wide and included zero; see \autoref{fig:specification_robustness_figure}.
This suggests there was not enough variation within countries in order to get a precise estimate without considering variation between countries.
Alternatively, I could use random effects or a pooled model.
The only advantage of using random effects is that a random effects model could result in more precise estimates.
In this case, using random effects resulted in less precise estimates; see \autoref{fig:filtered_specification_robustness_figure}.
Thus, for simplicity, I choose to use the pooled model.

I also had to decide what to do about variation in price of life observations within a country-year.
Some country-years had as many as 551 price of life observations, and others had none at all.
For the main model, as I discussed above, I simply averaged the log price of life observations within a country-year.
Alternately, to correctly take into account variation within a country-year, I created a hierarchical Bayesian model.
I specified this model as follows.
I standardized all regressors.
Instead of using modeled life expectancy in the second stage, I used observed life expectancy, and modeled the residuals as correlated between the two stages.
I used dummy variables for year fixed effects.
For each stage, I estimated random effects by country with non-centered parameterization, allowing correlation in the random effects between the two stages.
Finally, I modeled random variation around a true mean for log price of life observations.
I used standard normal priors for coefficients, exponential (1) priors for standard deviations, and LKJ (2) priors for correlation matrices.
As the central estimate from the Bayesian model was comparable to the estimate from the pooled model, I decided to use the pooled model for the main result for simplicity.

I also considered alternate sets of control variables.
For the ``with multicollinear controls'' model, I included the control variables that I excluded due to multicollinearity.
This results in a wider confidence interval; see \autoref{fig:filtered_specification_robustness_figure}.
For the ``significant'' controls, I only included statistically significant controls.
This results in a narrower confidence interval; see \autoref{fig:filtered_specification_robustness_figure}.
Neither set yields a significantly different central estimate, suggesting that none of the control variables considered are confounding the effect of life expectancy.

Finally, I considered ignoring endogeneity by using observed life expectancy directly as a regressor.
To do so means assuming that the slope of the marginal benefit of life curve is zero.
This results in a lower, but still statistically and practically significant, estimate; see \autoref{fig:filtered_specification_robustness_figure}.
This suggests that the slope of the marginal benefit of curve is negative, but not negative enough to outweigh the strongly positive slope of the marginal cost curve.

I clustered standard errors for all models except for the hierarchical Bayesian model by country.
Thus, standard errors should be robust to heteroskedasticity and within-country autocorrelation.

The results for all the alternatives are in \autoref{fig:specification_robustness_figure}.

\section{Two-stage least squares equations}
\label{sec:two_stage_least_squares_equations}


Here, I derive the equations used for a two-stage least squares estimation.
I define variables as follows:

\begin{itemize}

\item $P$: the log price of life, in dollars per QALY
\item $Q$: life expectancy in years
\item $I$: log GDP per capita, in dollars
\item $C$: a vector of control variables.
\item $\alpha_0$: the intercept term for the marginal benefit equation.
\item $\alpha_Q$: the coefficient for life expectancy in the marginal benefit equation.
\item $\alpha_I$: the coefficient for log GDP per capita in the marginal benefit equation.
\item $\alpha_C$: a vector of coefficients for control variables in the marginal benefit equation.
\item $\epsilon_{MB}$: the error term for the marginal benefit equation
\item $\beta_0$: the intercept for the marginal cost equation
\item $\beta_Q$: the coefficient for life expectancy in the marginal cost equation.
\item $\beta_C$: a vector of coefficients for control variables in the marginal cost equation.
\item $\epsilon_{MC}$: the error term for the marginal cost equation
\end{itemize}

The equation for the marginal benefit of life curve is as follows:

\[ P = \alpha_0 + \alpha_Q Q + \alpha_I I + \alpha_C \cdot C + \epsilon_{MB} \]

The equation for the marginal cost of life curve is as follows:

\[ P = \beta_0 + \beta_Q Q + \beta_C \cdot C + \epsilon_{MC} \]

I cannot use least squares to estimate $\beta_Q$ because $\epsilon_{MC}$ is correlated with $Q$.

However, if I solve for $Q$, I get an equation that I can estimate.

First, subtract the marginal cost equation from the marginal benefit equation:

\[ \alpha_0 - \beta_0 + (\alpha_Q - \beta_Q) Q + \alpha_I I + (\alpha_C - \beta_C) \cdot C + \epsilon_{MB} - \epsilon_{MC} = 0 \]

Now, solve for $Q$:

\[ Q = \frac{\alpha_0 - \beta_0}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} + \frac{\alpha_I}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} I + \frac{\alpha_C - \beta_C}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} \cdot C + \frac{\epsilon_{MB} - \epsilon_{MC}}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} \]

This is the equation I estimated in the first stage.

Then, 

\[ E(Q) = \frac{\alpha_0 - \beta_0}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} + \frac{\alpha_I}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} I + \frac{\alpha_C - \beta_C}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} \cdot C \]

So,

\[ Q = E(Q) - \frac{\epsilon_{MB} - \epsilon_{MC}}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} \]

Now, substituting this into the marginal cost equation:

\[ P = \beta_0 + \beta_Q \left(E(Q) - \frac{\epsilon_{MB} - \epsilon_{MC}}{\beta_Q - \alpha_Q} \right) + \beta_C \cdot C + \epsilon_{MC} \]
\[ = \beta_0 + \beta_Q E(Q) + \beta_C \cdot C + \beta_Q \epsilon_Q + \frac{\alpha_Q \epsilon_{MC} - \beta_Q \epsilon_{MB}}{\alpha_Q - \beta_Q} \]

This is the equation I estimated in the second stage.
